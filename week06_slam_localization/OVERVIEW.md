# Week 6 Lab Overview — SLAM and Localization

This individual ROS 2 lab asks students to operate, inspect, and evaluate established simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) and localization systems. Students do not implement SLAM algorithms from scratch. Instead, they learn what information the system uses, how operator behavior affects its output, how to save and reuse a map, and how to decide whether a robot’s pose estimate is trustworthy. The lab reuses the shared Docker environment established in Week 1.

In Mission 1, students teleoperate a simulated TurtleBot3 through an initially unknown environment while SLAM Toolbox processes LiDAR and odometry data. They observe the scan, pose estimate, transforms, and occupancy grid as the map evolves. Students plan an exploration route, save the completed map, and measure coverage and structural qualities such as clipping, fragmentation, duplicated walls, or misalignment. The mission emphasizes that a map is an inference produced from motion and measurements, not a perfect copy of the world.

Mission 2 examines mapping strategy. Each student maps the same environment again using a meaningfully different exploration pattern. They compare deliberate boundary following, broad coverage, revisiting, turning behavior, and loop closure. Quantitative map analysis and matched screenshots help students determine why one strategy produces better coverage or alignment. They must connect visible map defects to plausible causes instead of judging maps only by appearance.

In Mission 3, students save the selected map, restart the robot, and run AMCL localization against it. Controlled trials include a good initial pose, an incorrect initial pose, an ambiguous location, and degraded scan data. Students record estimated and reference poses, calculate or interpret localization error, and analyze convergence and recovery. They consider when a concentrated estimate is justified and when similar geometry, weak sensing, or an incorrect initialization should cause the system or operator to remain uncertain.

Students submit map image and metadata files, exploration plans, screenshots or ROS evidence, map-quality measurements, localization trial records, error analysis, and a final explanation of what it means for a robot to “know where it is.” All work and submission are individual. By the end, students should understand the roles of LiDAR, odometry, transforms, loop closure, occupancy grids, and particle-based localization; operate a standard ROS mapping workflow; compare exploration strategies; and evaluate localization as an uncertain estimate rather than a binary success indicator.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
