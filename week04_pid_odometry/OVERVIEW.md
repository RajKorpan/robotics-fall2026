# Week 4 Lab Overview — PID Control and Odometry

This individual, self-contained Streamlit lab develops intuition for feedback control and differential-drive odometry. Students manipulate a simulated robot, observe plots and motion replays, and use quantitative performance measures to justify engineering choices. ROS 2 is not required for this lab; removing middleware and simulator setup keeps attention on how control gains change behavior and how motion estimates accumulate error.

Students first establish an open-loop or baseline response. They observe what happens when a robot attempts to follow a target without a well-tuned feedback controller and identify error, delayed response, overshoot, oscillation, and steady-state behavior. This baseline becomes the reference for later comparisons. Students are expected to distinguish what they see in a replay from what the plotted error and recorded trial data establish.

The PID portion asks students to vary proportional, integral, and derivative gains deliberately. Rather than searching randomly for a combination that passes, they change one factor at a time, predict the consequence, and compare runs. They investigate how proportional action reacts to present error, integral action accumulates persistent error, and derivative action responds to the rate of change. Their selected controller must be defended using measures such as settling time, overshoot, oscillation, and final error. The goal is to understand the trade-offs of a controller, not to discover a universally perfect set of gains.

The odometry portion connects wheel motion to estimated robot pose. Students compare the desired or actual trajectory with the trajectory reconstructed from wheel measurements. They manipulate or observe effects such as wheel-radius mismatch, track-width error, timing error, and measurement noise. Small local errors may appear harmless at first but accumulate into visible position and heading drift. Students also connect control quality to estimation: oscillatory or poorly tracked motion can make the resulting trajectory harder to predict and verify.

Each student submits controller settings, baseline and PID trial data, plots, odometry data, written predictions and explanations, and GIF evidence of the interactive work. The generated `student_submission/` folder contains structured JSON, CSV, images, explanations, and a manifest and is committed through the student’s own repository. By the end, students should be able to explain the roles of P, I, and D; evaluate a controller with evidence; derive the purpose of odometry; and explain why dead-reckoned pose estimates drift.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
