# Week 11 Lab Overview — Human–Robot Interaction Evaluation

This lab applies a complete human-centered evaluation cycle to a working ROS 2 interaction: prototype, user test, evidence, redesign, and retest. The implementation, records, analysis, and submission are individual. Students temporarily work in pairs only so that each person can serve as the other’s participant; each student evaluates their own system and submits only their own non-identifying data. The shared Docker environment provides the ROS interaction node, observable topics, and Streamlit guide.

Mission 1 asks students to inspect and dry-run a stateful robot interaction. The robot communicates its intent, approaches, listens for a request, confirms consequential interpretations, acts, reports status, handles timeouts, accepts correction or cancellation, and exposes an emergency stop. Students trace states and ROS messages and verify ordinary and failure paths. They examine whether visible or textual feedback accurately communicates when the robot is listening, thinking, moving, waiting, or unable to continue.

In Mission 2, a peer completes five scripted baseline scenarios while the student acts as evaluator. Evidence includes task outcome, comprehension, feedback clarity, recovery from error, predictability, timing, and possible accessibility barriers. The participant is identified only by a random code; students do not collect names, video, voice recordings, demographic profiles, medical information, or unnecessary personal data. The evaluator avoids coaching except where the protocol explicitly permits it and distinguishes direct observation from interpretation.

Mission 3 converts findings into design changes. Each student identifies evidence-backed problems, implements at least two changes to configuration, feedback, timing, state transitions, or accessible interaction options, and repeats the same five scenarios. Holding the task set—and the participant when practical—constant supports a meaningful baseline-versus-redesign comparison. Students must report improvements, regressions, and remaining limitations rather than selecting only favorable observations.

The individual submission includes prototype configuration, state diagram or ROS trace, baseline and redesign records, checked metrics, non-identifying observation notes, design-change rationale, matched comparisons, screenshots, mission explanations, final synthesis, and a manifest. Students inspect the commit for identifying data before pushing. By the end, they should be able to evaluate whether users understand a robot’s intentions and state, recognize feedback and recovery failures, document accessibility barriers, translate observations into concrete redesigns, and use a controlled retest to determine whether the changes actually improved the interaction.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
