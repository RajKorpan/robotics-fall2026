# Week 10 Lab Overview — Foundation Models for Robotics

This individual, self-contained Streamlit lab examines how language and vision-language foundation models can contribute to robotics without being granted unchecked control. The emphasis is capability versus reliability, not model architecture or training. Students work with a controlled, reproducible response environment so everyone can test the same ambiguities, hallucinations, unsafe requests, and visual failures without depending on a changing external model service.

Mission 1 focuses on translating natural-language requests into robot plans. Students evaluate six requests and preserve the model’s original action sequences. They determine whether each plan is executable and grounded in the robot’s allowed capabilities, whether prerequisites are missing, and whether ambiguity should trigger clarification. Students identify hallucinated actions and implicit assumptions rather than repairing the output silently. This separates the model’s plausible language from an engineering claim that a plan can actually run.

Mission 2 introduces vision and language through eight controlled scenes. Conditions include ambiguity, occlusion, low light, unusual objects, misleading context, and partial views. Students compare model descriptions, object claims, confidence, and recommended actions with available evidence. They analyze false confidence and determine when a recommendation should be treated as uncertain even if it is expressed fluently. The goal is to understand that multimodal output remains an inference shaped by both visible information and learned expectations.

Mission 3 asks students to design an independent verification layer. They define an allowed action set, prerequisite and parameter rules, uncertainty handling, human-confirmation boundaries, and safe fallback behavior. Twelve cases test unsafe instructions, conflicting goals, unavailable actions, consequential decisions, and ambiguous intent. A robust architecture may reject a plan, request clarification, abstain, or route a decision to a human rather than forcing execution.

Students submit the preserved model outputs, capability and grounding analyses, scene-level evidence, verifier configuration, test results, mission explanations, and final architecture rationale. All work is individual. By the end, students should be able to evaluate language-to-plan and vision-language output, distinguish fluency from reliability, identify missing authority and prerequisites, and design a robotics architecture in which a foundation model proposes actions while deterministic software and people retain appropriate verification and control.
