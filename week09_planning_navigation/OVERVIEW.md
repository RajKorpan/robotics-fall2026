# Week 9 Lab Overview — Planning and Human-Aware Navigation

This individual ROS 2 lab builds on mapping, localization, and perception by asking students to use Navigation2 to plan and execute autonomous motion. Students do not implement an industrial navigation stack. They learn to inspect its plans, measure execution, recognize impossible goals, and redesign navigation rules when geometric collision avoidance is not sufficient for socially appropriate behavior. The lab reuses the shared Docker environment and a validated map from Week 6.

In Mission 1, students predict and request paths to five goal poses. The cases include short routes, routes requiring obstacle clearance or detours, and invalid or unreachable goals. Students inspect generated paths and compare length, minimum clearance, shape, and rejection behavior. They distinguish a path planner’s geometric output from the robot’s eventual execution and explain how the map, footprint, inflation, and goal placement affect feasibility.

Mission 2 asks the robot to navigate autonomously through a controlled set of trials. Students measure success, completion time, traveled distance, recoveries, collisions, and near misses. Repeated, structured evidence matters more than a single demonstration. Students compare the planned path with executed motion and diagnose failures involving localization, controller behavior, clearance, or recovery. This mission establishes that “a path exists” and “the robot navigated successfully” are different claims.

Mission 3 introduces simulated people and scenario-specific personal-space regions. Students first document a baseline path that is technically collision-free but violates a stated human-aware requirement—for example, passing unacceptably close to a seated person. They then modify at least two costmap, inflation, mask, or behavioral rules and retest the same start and goal. The redesign must balance social clearance with feasibility, path length, and navigation performance rather than solving the problem by making all routes impossible.

Students submit predictions, requested and generated paths, navigation metrics, baseline and redesigned configuration, personal-space evidence, matched trial comparisons, explanations, and a 200–300 word synthesis. All configuration and analysis are individual. By the end, students should be able to distinguish planning from execution, evaluate navigation quantitatively, interpret failure and recovery, and explain why socially successful navigation requires contextual costs and constraints beyond physical collision avoidance.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
