# Week 8 Lab Overview — Computer Vision and Learned Perception

This individual ROS 2 lab compares a classical computer-vision pipeline with a frozen pretrained learned detector and then connects uncertain visual perception to bounded robot behavior. Students are not asked to train a substantial model. Instead, they investigate how two different perception approaches produce detections, confidence, false positives, and false negatives under controlled environmental changes. The shared Week 1 Docker environment provides ROS, OpenCV, the camera pipeline, and the Streamlit guide.

Mission 1 uses color segmentation, thresholding, morphology, contours, and simple geometric features. Students tune one pipeline for an assigned target and then hold those settings fixed while testing multiple conditions. This restriction prevents condition-by-condition retuning from hiding brittleness. Students inspect intermediate masks and final detections and quantify performance as lighting, distance, occlusion, orientation, and background change. They identify the assumptions that make a classical detector interpretable and efficient but also fragile.

Mission 2 repeats the evaluation using a documented frozen pretrained model. Students inspect predicted classes, bounding boxes, and confidence values and sweep a confidence threshold without training the network. They calculate or interpret false-positive and false-negative behavior across the same condition bank. The comparison asks where learned features improve robustness, where confidence is misleading, and where model capability depends on training categories and deployment context. Neither method is presented as automatically superior.

Mission 3 connects the camera-perception output to a decision and motion node. The robot searches for, centers on, approaches, and stops for a target while respecting bounded velocities, minimum confidence, stop area, and stale-data fallback. Students trace the complete ROS graph from camera to perception to decision to guarded command output. They test target loss, uncertain detection, stale messages, and stopping, not only a successful approach.

Students submit fixed classical settings, learned-detector threshold experiments, condition-level metrics, ROS/system diagrams, behavioral test evidence, screenshots, explanations, and a final account of when the robot’s apparent ability to “see” breaks down. The submission remains individual and preserves both successes and failures. By the end, students should be able to compare classical and learned perception fairly, interpret detection confidence cautiously, evaluate environmental brittleness, and design behavior that remains safe when visual evidence is absent or uncertain.
