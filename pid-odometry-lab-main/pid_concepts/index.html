PID Control

Stop the car at the line.

Consider a car approaching a stop sign. It needs to stop at the line -- not short of it, and not past it into the crosswalk. Something decides, moment by moment, how much gas to apply and when to ease off. That something is the controller.

First, you'll act as the controller. You'll drive the car toward the line by hand to see why doing this consistently is difficult. Then we'll build a formula that does it smoothly, one term at a time.

First idea

Two ways to control a system

This distinction underlies the rest of the lab, so it's worth naming up front.

Open loop
Command without feedback.

The action is decided in advance -- for example, "apply gas for 2 seconds, then release." The controller never measures the result. If conditions differ from what was assumed (an incline, a low battery, a heavier load), the car stops in the wrong place and the controller has no way to detect it.

Closed loop (feedback)
Command based on measurement.

The controller continuously measures the remaining gap to the line and adjusts: reducing gas as it approaches, applying more if it stalls short. Because it responds to the actual state, it corrects for conditions it wasn't told about. PID is a closed-loop controller.

In the next step you'll act as a closed-loop controller, watching the gap and correcting in real time. Notice how difficult it is to do consistently -- that difficulty motivates using a formula instead.

The problem

Your corrections were inconsistent

A formula can replace the guesswork. Rather than estimating each adjustment by hand, a controller converts the error (the distance from the car to the line) directly into a command. There are several ways to define that conversion.
Kinds of controller

A controller is a rule from error to action

Every controller answers the same question -- given the current error, what command should be applied? -- but each uses a different rule. Here are three, from simplest to most capable.

Bang-bang (on/off)

Full command, or none.

Below the target, apply full power; at or above it, apply none. This is how a home thermostat or a refrigerator operates. It is simple to implement, but because it never eases in, it tends to overshoot and oscillate around the target.

Proportional (P)

Command proportional to the error.

A large error produces a large command; a small error produces a small one. This is smoother than on/off control and is the basis of PID. On its own, however, P can still overshoot and may settle slightly short of the target.

PID

Proportional, derivative, integral.

Add a D term that responds to how quickly the error is changing (damping the approach) and an I term that accumulates residual error (eliminating a steady offset). Together the three terms settle accurately on the target.

See the simplest one in action first. A bang-bang controller applies full command toward the target whenever it's off, and nothing once it's close -- so it never settles, it chatters back and forth across the line.

Why not use bang-bang everywhere? Because applying full command until arrival means arriving at full speed. Systems that must settle precisely -- a car at a stop sign, a robot arm, a drone, a heater near its setpoint -- need to ease in as the error shrinks. The rest of this tutorial builds that behavior: P, then D, then I.
Proportional control

P reacts to the current error

The simplest smooth idea: command = Kp * error. Big error means big push, small error means small push. The stop line is again the edge of a cliff -- overshooting goes over. Watch what happens with Kp = 2.0.

P overshoots -- and here that means going over the edge. It applies a large command when the error is large, but it has no awareness of how fast the error is closing. The car still carries speed when it reaches the line, so it crosses the edge. This calls for a term that acts as a brake.
Add derivative

D brakes when approaching fast

command = Kp * error + Kd * d(error)/dt. The D term looks at how fast the error is changing. When the robot rushes toward the target, error shrinks fast -- so D pulls back, like a brake. Watch with Kp = 2.0, Kd = 1.0.

Much smoother! D acts like a brake -- when the error is shrinking fast, D pulls back. The robot approaches the target without wild oscillation. But what if there's a constant force pushing the robot off-target? P and D alone can't handle that.
Full PID

I accumulates past error to eliminate offset

A steady wind pushes the car left, away from the line. P and D get it close but can't close the gap -- the wind exactly balances the P correction, leaving a steady-state error (the car stops short). The I term fixes this: command = Kp * error + Ki * integral(error) + Kd * d(error)/dt. Over time, I accumulates the leftover error and pushes harder until the car reaches the line. Watch with Kp = 2.0, Ki = 0.3, Kd = 1.4.

I closes the gap. When the robot is stuck slightly off-target, the integral of error builds up over time, producing an ever-growing push that eventually overcomes the disturbance. In the full controller, P responds to current error, D adds damping, and I removes residual offset.
Summary

PID control summary

Measure the error, respond proportionally, add damping as the target is approached, and accumulate residual error to remove any offset. Three gains -- Kp, Ki, Kd -- define the controller. You'll tune them yourself in the next part.

The same PID structure is used in systems such as thermostats, cruise control, drone stabilization, and industrial process control. The gains determine how the controller responds to error and must be selected for the dynamics and requirements of each system.
