# Week 12 Lab Overview — Responsible Robotics by Design

This individual, self-contained Streamlit lab treats responsible robotics as an engineering activity. It differs from an ethics audit: students do not stop after naming privacy, fairness, safety, accessibility, or control concerns. They alter a configurable service-robot architecture, run explicit scenarios and checks, observe trade-offs, and revise the design until its behavior satisfies measurable requirements. The lab uses reproducible simulations rather than ROS 2 so students can concentrate on policy-to-behavior connections.

Mission 1 addresses privacy by design. Students decide what sensor data is collected, whether processing is local or remote, what is logged, who can access records, how long data is retained, how consent works, and whether information can be deleted or anonymized. The robot must still determine when assistance may be needed, but students challenge unnecessary collection—for example, whether recognizing a possible need requires storing raw video. Automated cases expose designs whose written intent sounds privacy-preserving but whose actual configuration retains or transmits excessive data.

Mission 2 examines unequal perception performance across four user or environmental conditions. Students inspect confusion metrics and test interventions such as adjusted thresholds, additional calibration data, an alternate sensing modality, abstention under uncertainty, and human review. They compare subgroup false-positive and false-negative rates as well as overall usefulness. The objective is not to produce a cosmetic statement about avoiding bias; it is to show how an intervention changes who receives errors, where the system declines to act, and what operational burden follows.

Mission 3 combines physical safety, accessibility, and human control. Students configure speed near people, personal-space distance, minimum confidence, emergency stopping, confirmation for consequential actions, redundant feedback and command modalities, and local fallback behavior. Scenario checks include degraded sensing, communication loss, access needs, ambiguous intent, and urgent stopping. Some seemingly convenient configurations fail, requiring revision and justification.

Students submit all three policy configurations, scenario results, subgroup metrics, requirement checks, explanations, trade-off analysis, revision evidence, and a final responsible-system rationale. All work is individual. By the end, students should be able to translate responsible-design principles into data flows, thresholds, interfaces, safeguards, and fallback policies; measure the consequences of interventions; and explain why responsible robotics requires ongoing technical verification rather than promises in a design document.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
