# Week 3 Lab Overview — Robot Motion, Frames, and AI-Assisted Programming

This individual ROS 2 lab develops precise reasoning about mobile-robot motion and coordinate frames while introducing a disciplined process for AI-assisted software development. Students move beyond issuing velocity commands and begin predicting pose, interpreting observations in different frames, and establishing program correctness with evidence. The lab reuses the shared ROS 2 Docker environment configured in Week 1.

In the first mission, students analyze combinations of linear velocity, angular velocity, and duration before running them. They predict whether a command produces straight motion, rotation in place, or an arc and calculate an expected final pose. They then execute controlled trials, record odometry, and compare predicted and observed results. The purpose is not to obtain perfect agreement; it is to identify and explain the assumptions behind the motion model, including constant velocity, integration over time, heading conventions, command startup and stopping, and simulator measurements.

The second mission focuses on frames and transformations. Students distinguish the world or odometry frame from the robot base and sensor frames. They interpret points expressed relative to a sensor, transform or reason about those points in another frame, and explain why the same coordinates have different meanings depending on their frame. Students use ROS TF tools and visual evidence to trace relationships rather than treating frame names as abstract notation. This mission prepares them for mapping, perception, and navigation, where frame mistakes can create plausible but incorrect behavior.

The final mission requires an AI assistant to help create an individually assigned motion-pattern node. AI use is required, but generated code is not accepted as proof of understanding. Students preserve the original prompt and response, identify unsupported assumptions or defects, modify the code, and design tests that address timing, stopping, command sequencing, parameter handling, and safe failure. They compare the first output with the final implementation and explain why the tests establish the intended behavior.

Students submit motion predictions, measured pose evidence, frame diagrams or traces, original AI output, a critique of that output, modifications, test cases and results, final ROS code, and a written correctness argument. The work is completed and submitted individually. By the end, students should be able to connect velocity commands to pose, reason explicitly about coordinate frames, and treat an AI assistant as a fallible development tool whose output must be reviewed and verified.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
