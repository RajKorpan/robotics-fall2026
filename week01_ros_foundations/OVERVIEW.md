# Week 1 Lab Overview: ROS 2 Foundations and Mobile Robot Exploration

This individual lab introduces ROS 2 by asking students to discover how a simulated mobile robot works as a system. The emphasis is not simply on installing software or writing a Python program. Students learn that a robot is composed of interacting processes that exchange structured messages: simulation produces sensor data, perception and behavior nodes interpret that data, and command nodes affect motion. Week 1 also includes the one-time setup of the shared Docker course environment used again in Weeks 3, 6, 8, 9, and 11. The setup is a prerequisite and is verified by the lab, but troubleshooting installation is not the central graded objective.

Before launching ROS, students complete three guided, ungraded visual tutorials. Part 1 provides manipulable toy examples of noisy or missing sensors, response delay, distributed-component failures, and wheel slip. Part 2 animates the same hospital-delivery scenario under reactive, behavior-based, deliberative, and hybrid architectures, including arbitration, replanning, and a local safety override. Part 3 lets students follow messages through a ROS graph, compare topic streams with service calls, break connections, and use simulated inspection commands. These parts teach through observation and experimentation rather than written quiz responses.

A guided preflight then confirms ROS 2 Jazzy, TurtleBot3, Gazebo, RViz, the course packages, and the Streamlit guide. Mission 1 begins by defining the ROS 2 graph, Gazebo, RViz, TurtleBot3 World, nodes, topics, publishers, and subscribers. Students then follow a guided tour of four named components and six narrowly focused terminal observations. Vocabulary boxes introduce LiDAR, odometry, Twist, `cmd_vel`, and TF before students encounter those terms in commands. Worked examples show the sensing path and the protected command path, including components that contribute to more than one part of sense, decide, and act. The live system does not require a service investigation. Three scaffolded responses ask students to explain the graph, the command guard, and the difference between simulation and visualization. The lab generates a system diagram from captured endpoints rather than assumed connections.

Mission 2 moves from observation to guided motion experiments. Animated top-down previews introduce forward speed, turning speed, duration, Twist messages, and the difference between straight, rotating, and curved motion. Students save one prediction and run one trial at a time. Each Run button resets Gazebo to the same starting state, executes a bounded ROS 2 command, sends a final zero command, and records evidence before the next trial opens. Students then use safe sliders to design a modified curve. A simplified table distinguishes commanded path length, odometry-estimated traveled path, straight-line displacement, direction change, and command timing. Three scaffolded explanations replace the earlier target-zone task and repetitive response set.

In Mission 3, students implement two focused Python functions inside a supplied ROS 2 behavior node. A worked LiDAR example teaches how scan indices correspond to angles and how to select the nearest valid reading in front of the robot. Students then implement a safe move-or-stop rule, create one unit test, build the package, run the node, and verify its subscriber and publisher connections. The evaluator tests clear-path, threshold, invalid-data, stale-data, and velocity-limit cases. Three focused explanations ask students to trace how sensor data becomes a command, explain why missing data causes a stop, and describe how their functions, the ROS wrapper, and the command guard work together.

Each student submits an activity-completion record, guided ROS observations, a ROS/system diagram, predictions and observations, timing records, machine-readable evidence, written explanations, and an integrated 300–500 word synthesis. The submission must show both what happened and why the evidence supports the student’s claims. By the end, students should be able to explain why robotics software is difficult, compare architecture styles, describe ROS 2 as middleware, inspect a small ROS system with guidance, safely command a mobile robot, and situate a tested reactive node within a layered system.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
