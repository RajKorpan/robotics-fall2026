# Week 1 Lab Overview — ROS 2 Foundations and Mobile Robot Exploration

This individual lab introduces ROS 2 by asking students to discover how a simulated mobile robot works as a system. The emphasis is not simply on installing software or writing a Python program. Students learn that a robot is composed of interacting processes that exchange structured messages: simulation produces sensor data, perception and behavior nodes interpret that data, and command nodes affect motion. Week 1 also includes the one-time setup of the shared Docker course environment used again in Weeks 3, 6, 8, 9, and 11. The setup is a prerequisite and is verified by the lab, but troubleshooting installation is not the central graded objective.

Before launching ROS, students complete three guided, ungraded visual tutorials. Part 1 provides manipulable toy examples of noisy or missing sensors, response delay, distributed-component failures, and wheel slip. Part 2 animates the same hospital-delivery scenario under reactive, behavior-based, deliberative, and hybrid architectures, including arbitration, replanning, and a local safety override. Part 3 lets students follow messages through a ROS graph, compare topic streams with service calls, break connections, and use simulated inspection commands. These parts teach through observation and experimentation rather than written quiz responses.

A guided preflight then confirms ROS 2 Jazzy, TurtleBot3, Gazebo, RViz, the course packages, and the Streamlit guide. In Mission 1, students observe the robot before changing it. They inspect active nodes, topics, message types, publishers/subscribers, and services. They classify live nodes by subsystem responsibility and by sense–decide–act role, then construct a system diagram showing sensing, command, simulation, visualization, and safety paths. This develops the ability to read a ROS graph as an implemented architecture rather than as a list of commands.

Mission 2 moves from observation to controlled experimentation. Students predict the effects of specified linear and angular velocities, publish commands, and compare straight, rotating, and curved motion with their predictions. Evidence records requested and actual command duration, zero-command time, expected linear travel, pose, and observed displacement. Students use these records to explain discrepancies and connect response delay with physical risk. Safety is built into the environment through bounded velocities, stale-command stopping, and explicit zero commands.

In Mission 3, students complete a small LiDAR-based behavior node. The node subscribes to range data, determines whether an obstacle is too close, and publishes a safe motion command. Students identify this stimulus–threshold–response design as reactive and test clear-path, threshold, invalid-data, and stale-data cases. They then explain its limited foresight, the need for arbitration among competing behaviors, how a planner could form a future hybrid system, why a command guard needs override authority, and why software limits do not replace a physical emergency stop.

Each student submits an activity-completion record, node, ROS/system diagram, architecture analysis, predictions and observations, service inspection, timing records, machine-readable evidence, written explanations, and an integrated 300–500 word synthesis. The submission must show both what happened and why the evidence supports the student’s claims. By the end, students should be able to explain why robotics software is difficult, compare architecture styles, describe ROS 2 as middleware, inspect an unfamiliar ROS system, safely command a mobile robot, and situate a tested reactive node within a layered system.

The submission ends with a required individual reflection of no more than 300 words. Students may use any of the course reflection prompts to connect the activity with their interests, motivation, human or societal considerations, and overall experience.
