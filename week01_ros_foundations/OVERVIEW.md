# Week 1 Lab Overview — ROS 2 Foundations and Mobile Robot Exploration

This individual lab introduces ROS 2 by asking students to discover how a simulated mobile robot works as a system. The emphasis is not simply on installing software or writing a Python program. Students learn that a robot is composed of interacting processes that exchange structured messages: simulation produces sensor data, perception and behavior nodes interpret that data, and command nodes affect motion. Week 1 also includes the one-time setup of the shared Docker course environment used again in Weeks 3, 6, 8, 9, and 11. The setup is a prerequisite and is verified by the lab, but troubleshooting installation is not the central graded objective.

Students begin with a guided preflight that confirms ROS 2 Jazzy, TurtleBot3, Gazebo, RViz, the course packages, and the Streamlit guide are available. In Mission 1, they observe the robot before changing it. They inspect active nodes and topics, identify message types and publisher/subscriber relationships, visualize sensor messages, and construct a system diagram that explains which components perform simulation, sensing, motion control, and safety enforcement. This develops the ability to read a ROS graph as an architecture rather than as a list of commands.

Mission 2 moves from observation to controlled experimentation. Students predict the effects of specified linear and angular velocities, publish commands, and compare the resulting straight, rotating, and curved motion with their predictions. They collect pose and command evidence and explain discrepancies using timing, coordinate conventions, simulation behavior, and command limits. Safety is built into the environment through bounded velocities, stale-command stopping, and explicit stopping procedures.

In Mission 3, students complete a small LiDAR-based behavior node. The node subscribes to range data, determines whether an obstacle is too close, and publishes a safe motion command. Students test clear-path, approaching-obstacle, stop, invalid-data, and stale-data cases rather than relying on a single successful demonstration. The final work connects the complete flow from sensor message to decision to robot action.

Each student submits their node, ROS/system diagram, predictions and observations, machine-readable evidence, screenshots or other simulation evidence, written explanations, and a final synthesis. The submission must show both what happened and why the evidence supports the student’s claims. By the end of the lab, students should be able to inspect an unfamiliar ROS system, safely command a mobile robot, implement a simple reactive node, and explain the robot as a network of interacting components.
