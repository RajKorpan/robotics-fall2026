# Week 14 Lab Overview — Reinforcement Learning with an Inverted Pendulum

This individual Streamlit lab uses the CartPole inverted-pendulum problem to make reinforcement learning visible and experimentally testable. Students compare tabular Q-learning and Deep Q-learning (DQN), manipulate rewards, observations, actions, and starting conditions, and connect training curves to the actual physical behavior shown in replay GIFs. The lab is self-contained and does not require ROS 2.

Students begin with tabular Q-learning. Continuous observations must be converted into discrete buckets, making representation choices explicit. Students inspect exploration, learned action values, returns, and evaluation rollouts and consider what information is lost through discretization. They alter a clearly located reward function and predict how rewarding angle, position, survival, or corrective behavior may change what the agent learns. A higher training return is not sufficient by itself; students compare learning curves with a replay of the resulting policy.

The observation and action experiments show that the learning problem is partly defined by the designer. Students may hide velocity, replace angle with sine and cosine, add a zero-force action, or fix the initial state. They analyze how missing information can make states difficult to distinguish, how alternate representations change continuity, and how action choices and starting-state diversity affect the policy. These experiments reinforce that an agent’s apparent intelligence depends on the signals and options provided to it.

Students then compare tabular learning with DQN. The neural agent can approximate values over continuous observations instead of relying on a finite table, but it introduces optimization choices, instability, and less directly inspectable representations. Students use the value-map and evaluation tools to compare what the two methods appear to have learned and discuss whether performance differences follow from representation, training budget, randomness, or algorithm design. Small smoke-test configurations support quick checks, while graded runs require meaningful evidence.

The app automatically saves written responses and the latest real training artifacts. Each student submits response records, training configuration, evaluation metrics, replay GIF, learning-curve CSV, checked mission records, explanations, and a final comparison in `student_submission/`. By the end, students should be able to explain state, action, reward, exploration, and value; describe the Q-learning update conceptually; compare tabular and function-approximation approaches; and evaluate a learned controller using both numerical performance and observed physical behavior.
